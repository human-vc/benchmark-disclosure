\section{Data and Setting}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The disclosure problem}\label{sec:data-model}

To begin, we assume release $i$ occurs at time $t_i$ and contains a single document. Benchmarks are indexed by $b \in \mathcal{B}$. They are released at time $\tau_b$. At the time of the writing of release $i$ the provider is endowed with results for a set of benchmarks $E_i \subseteq \mathcal{B}$. The provider chooses to disclose scores $s_{ib}$ for $b \in D_i \subseteq E_i$. The receiver observes $D_i$ and the scores, and must guess whether the remainder of the set represents withheld results, or non-existent results.

When endowment $E_i$ is common knowledge, selective disclosure is dominated, as non-disclosure reveals the worst-case scenario and disclosure unravels with $D_i = E_i$ \citep{grossman1981,milgrom1981}. This breaks down in at least two ways: (i) when $E_i$ is common knowledge but disclosing results can be costly, leading to a cutoff strategy in $s_{ib}$ \citep{verrecchia1983}; and (ii) when the receiver is unable to distinguish between non-existence and withholding, in that the sender can show evidence of having a result but not evidence of non-existence \citep[Section 3]{dye1985}.

In our setting an independent evaluator reports scores for cells that have been passed over by the provider, and each $\tau_b$ makes non-existence verifiable. Denote the available set and scored cells by $A_i = \{b : \tau_b < t_i\}$ and $O_i$; then
\[
D_i \subseteq E_i \subseteq A_i, \qquad \theta = \Pr\!\left(b \notin D_i \;\middle|\; b \in O_i \cap A_i\right).
\]
In general providers may have results which are not produced by the evaluator, and benchmarks may exist but not be run, so $\theta$ is calculated with reference to the set of cells covered by the evaluator, and not the actual endowment, leaving the \citet{dye1985} friction in place within $A_i$.

In practice, assessment of whether results have been withheld requires a notion of standing $P_{ib}$, since scores are not commensurable across benchmarks. The design requires that, conditional on innocent concerns, availability not be an argument in the determination of $P_{ib}$ \citep{heckman1979}. A date rule cannot satisfy this, as $t_i - \tau_b$, $t_i$ and $\tau_b$ are perfectly collinear. If any two of these arguments determine $P_{ib}$, then the remaining argument cannot be excluded from $P_{ib}$ as a determining argument, which is the case here (Section~\ref{sec:core}; Appendix~\ref{sec:supporting} bounds it).

Two consequences of this follow which need to be kept apart: (i) to measure $\theta$, we require labels for disclosure, which we code in Section~\ref{sec:reach}; and (ii) interpretation of estimates of $\theta$ as measures of withholding runs into $A_i$ itself. Because availability is deterministically a step function of benchmark maturity, given by $\mathbf{1}[t_i - \tau_b > 0]$, no cells of equal maturity sit on opposite sides of the availability test. If standing is affected by maturity, then conditioning densely to make availability excludable violates positivity, which precludes global identification of selective disclosure using any metric of pool-relative standing, be it a win rate or percentile. Any scale for standing learned jointly will be subject to the same selection process which determines existence. Under assumptions of continuity, only the discontinuity at the threshold can be interpreted.

\subsection{The independent score set}\label{sec:data-scores}

We work with a pinned version of Epoch's benchmarking hub scraped on August 17th, 2026. This data includes 819 model-versions. Since we are interested in release-level disclosure, we use organisation, model and release date to identify releases. Epoch uses a version attribute to distinguish different scaffolds within the same release, e.g.\ different reasoning effort or context length variants. Since the provider publishes one document for one release, we collapse these versions to releases, taking the maximum score among scaffolds and retaining the spread as a check. We end up with a dataset with 353 releases, 74 benchmarks and 3,082 release-benchmark cells that have an associated independent score. The data is clustered by provider.

\subsection{Eligibility and benchmark dates}\label{sec:data-gate}

Since providers can only choose to exclude a benchmark if it exists, we gather publication dates for benchmarks to select on temporal eligibility: the earliest confirmable publication date (taken from Epoch when available; otherwise from the primary benchmark source when we need to map preprint versions, code versions and publication versions to an earliest public date). We flag as eligible only those cells where the benchmark publication date strictly predates the release date, and only when the benchmark publication date is confirmed. We restrict on the assumption that providers cannot exploit unpublicised benchmarks, an assumption that would be violated if the provider was privileged with pre-publication access to the benchmark. These violations reduce, rather than inflate, the disclosure gap: they shift eligible cells into postdating cells. They are invisible in our disclosure codes: no coded document reports a pre-publication benchmark. Our restrictions leave 2,355 of the 3,082 cells as eligible. We group separately those 476 cells where the benchmark strictly postdates the release date, cells that were never reportable. We exclude, rather than impute, cells where we cannot pin a benchmark date; errors in dates reduce our estimated boundary constructed in Section~\ref{sec:core}. In comparing releases, we make no minimum threshold for the number of benchmarks. This would drop the releases with only one eligible cell and one postdating cell. 57 of 146 releases with eligible and postdating cells have fewer than eight scored benchmarks. The more trivial impediment that the number of reportable benchmarks is determined by a release's date is taken up in Appendix~\ref{sec:opportunity}.

We follow the Outcome Reporting Bias In Trials (ORBIT) classification to code disclosure. ORBIT is standardised for use in clinical trials, where there is an extensive literature on measuring disappearing outcomes (those stated in a protocol but not published) \citep{chan2004,kirkham2010,dwan2013}. Where a cell exists, we code reporting (a number), citation (the benchmark is referenced but not reported) and variants (an alternative is reported); where a cell is missing, we code whether it was previously reported by another member of the same family, where it was reported by contemporaries, and where it was not reported by any similar provider. Our queue contains 108 releases (103 where we have eligible cells in our pinned dataset and five from a more recent vintage); we code the 100 where we can find a document at the time of release, for a total of 1,280 cells. No pre-Section~\ref{sec:reach} analysis relies on coded disclosure.

\subsection{Standing}\label{sec:data-standing}

Since raw performance on benchmarks is not directly comparable, we report relative performance by benchmark in percentiles, by ranking a model against those released within 182 days before or after the release date of the focal release (the ``window''), breaking ties with midranks. We report the total number of peers, and the fraction of the peers in the window that are released after the focal model. This metric is what windowed ordering implies, and we show it to suffer bias that resembles selective disclosure.

\subsection{Inference}\label{sec:data-inference}
One must similarly be careful with inferences. While there are 46 nominal provider clusters, 24 of these clusters contribute fewer than ten cells, 20 are single-release providers and the top five clusters account for 68.1\% of the sample. Where we have fewer than twelve clusters, we do not report analytic standard errors, since only above this threshold can the asymptotic distribution of cluster-robust standard errors be relied upon \citep{cameron2008}. Where we have fewer than twelve clusters, we rely on inference for regression coefficients using the restricted wild cluster bootstrap with 9{,}999 replications, imposing the null and resampling the Rademacher weights at the provider level. $P$-values for release-level means are from a cluster sign-flip randomisation test with 9{,}999 iterations. The cluster sign-flip test flips the contribution of each organisation, respecting correlation within providers (but not within benchmarks, which is corrected in the two-way clustered regressions). Confidence intervals are constructed from percentile bootstraps with 10,000 resamples at the provider level. In both, we construct $p$-values from $(k+1)/(P+1)$, where $k$ counts the draws at least as extreme as the observed statistic and $P$ the total iterations. Since symmetry leaves only $2^{G-1}$ distinct statistics for $G$ clusters, at least fifteen clusters are needed for a $p$-value precision of $0.0001$; the 18 headline organisations clear it.
