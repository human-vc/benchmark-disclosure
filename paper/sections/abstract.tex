\begin{abstract}
Model providers choose which benchmark results appear in a release document, and an
independent evaluator scores the same models on many more. What a provider left out becomes
examinable only with a bound on what it could have reported, and benchmark publication
dates supply one: an instrument built after a model shipped was not publicly available to it. The comparison this invites, between benchmarks that were available
and benchmarks that did not yet exist, fails as a check on itself. The second group already stands 12.23 percentile
points below the first, computed without a single disclosure label. Balancing the window's two sides cuts that to 9.14 points,
and the uncorrected gap stays positive across all 16 window-width and
benchmark-count specifications. The date deciding
whether a benchmark could have been reported also fixes where a model sits inside that
benchmark's coverage, an age-period-cohort collinearity. The same failure appears on a
leaderboard we did not build: across 14 HELM Lite releases a fixed set of models keeps
bit-identical scores, every published win rate moves and five pairs reverse order, while a
companion leaderboard behind an absolute headline shows none of it. Under three sign restrictions the
unidentified linear component accounts for at most 24 to 34\% of the availability gap across
specifications. Hand-coding every locatable release document, 100 of 108, at a second-coder $\kappa$ of 0.95 on reported versus not and 0.85 over all nine categories, makes the danger concrete: the coded omitted-versus-unavailable gap is $+11.4$ points, significant against zero, yet short, in every specification, of its matched label-free counterpart by roughly the wedge composition implies, while reported benchmarks stand 8.7 points above omitted eligible ones within release ($p = 0.0001$).
\end{abstract}
