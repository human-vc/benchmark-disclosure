\begin{abstract}
Model providers choose which benchmark results appear in a release document, and an
independent evaluator scores the same models on many more. What a provider left out
becomes examinable only with a bound on what it could have reported. Benchmark
publication dates supply one, since an instrument built after a model shipped was never
available to it. The comparison this invites, between benchmarks a provider passed over
and benchmarks that did not yet exist, fails as a check on itself. Across 146 releases
the second group already stands 12.23 percentile points below the first, computed
without a single disclosure label. Balancing the window's two sides cuts that to 8.69
points, and all sixteen specifications we ran leave it positive. The date deciding
whether a benchmark could have been reported also fixes where a model sits inside that
benchmark's coverage, an age-period-cohort collinearity. The same failure appears on a
leaderboard we did not build: across fourteen HELM Lite releases, 24 models keep
bit-identical scores. Every published win rate moves and five of their pairs reverse
order, while a companion leaderboard behind an absolute headline shows none of it. We
report no estimate of how much selective disclosure occurs, because measuring it needs a
measure of standing that timing does not already determine.
\end{abstract}
