\begin{abstract}
Model providers choose which benchmark results appear in a release document, and an
independent evaluator scores the same models on many more. What a provider left out
becomes examinable only with a bound on what it could have reported. Benchmark
publication dates supply one, since an instrument built after a model shipped was never
available to it. The comparison this invites, between benchmarks that were available
and benchmarks that did not yet exist, fails as a check on itself. The second group already stands 12.23 percentile
points below the first, computed without a single disclosure label. Balancing the window's two sides cuts that to 8.69
points, and sixteen choices of window width and benchmark-count minimum all leave it positive. The date deciding
whether a benchmark could have been reported also fixes where a model sits inside that
benchmark's coverage, an age-period-cohort collinearity. The same failure appears on a
leaderboard we did not build: across fourteen HELM Lite releases a fixed set of
models keeps bit-identical scores. Every published win rate moves and five of their pairs reverse
order, while a companion leaderboard behind an absolute headline shows none of it. We
report no estimate of how much selective disclosure occurs. Under three sign restrictions the
unidentified linear component accounts for at most 24 to 34\% of that gap across specifications.
\end{abstract}
