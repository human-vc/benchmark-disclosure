\section{Absence Is Not Omission}\label{sec:core}

\subsection{What the placebo comparison measures}\label{sec:core-placebo}

A study of this kind compares how a release stands on the benchmarks its provider reported against how it stands on the benchmarks it passed over, and the second set mixes results held back with results never held. Separating them calls for a control group of cells whose endowment is known to be empty, the object endowment uncertainty hides \citep{dye1985}, and the temporal gate appears to supply one. Some benchmarks in a release's comparison window did not exist when the model shipped, so those cells fall outside the release's endowment: a provider cannot report a score on an instrument not yet written. They are non-disclosures with nothing behind them, while the eligible cells, benchmarks that existed and went unreported, are non-disclosures in which silence can be strategic. Whatever makes an unreported benchmark look bad for innocent reasons, whether difficulty drift, saturation, or a shift in what the field measures, should press on both sets alike, so the difference between them should be zero under every innocent explanation and negative only under concealment. It requires no disclosure labels, which is what makes it attractive as a check.

It is not zero. Across 146 releases with both kinds of cell, eligible benchmarks outscore postdating ones by 12.23 percentile points on average, with a median of 10.69, and the gap is positive in 78.1\% of releases. Nothing in that calculation knows which benchmarks were reported. A measure that balances the two sides of the window, described below, cuts the figure to 8.69 points and 72.5\% of 142 releases without removing it. A cluster sign-flip randomization test over the 18 organisations holding both kinds of cell puts that mean at $p = 0.0001$, the smallest value 9{,}999 sign assignments can return. Cells no provider could have withheld already stand lower than cells it could, so the group built to hold the innocent explanations fixed does not, and is not a control.

\subsection{Where the gap comes from}\label{sec:core-boundary}

\autoref{tab:decomposition} locates the failure. Every row regresses within-benchmark standing on a placebo indicator, equal to one when the benchmark postdates the release, inside release fixed effects, so the release's own capability level is differenced out throughout, with provider-clustered standard errors over 46 organisations and $n = 2831$ cells. With release effects alone the placebo coefficient is $-13.443$ (1.238). Adding benchmark fixed effects absorbs 63.8\% of it and leaves $-4.869$ (1.260). Benchmark composition therefore carries most of the gap, and structurally so: the panel is unbalanced, and placebo cells concentrate by construction in benchmarks that entered late. Those benchmarks are less saturated: splitting the 54 benchmarks carrying ten or more scores at their median publication date, 8.3\% of scores on the later half sit in the top decile of that benchmark's own range against 13.2\% on the earlier half ($p = 0.015$), so a late instrument leaves more room below the ceiling and every model on it stands lower. Adding the peer-window asymmetry term, the share of a cell's comparison window released after the focal model, instead of benchmark effects absorbs 29.2\% and leaves $-9.515$ (1.254). Peer count entered alone leaves $-2.566$ (1.305), but it acts as a suppressor and its apparent power is not interpretable alone, since including it drives the eligible-only asymmetry slope from $-29.09$ to $-42.83$. Conditioning jointly on benchmark effects and both window terms absorbs 98.2\% and leaves $+0.246$ with a standard error of 1.618 and a $t$ of $+0.152$, which is not distinguishable from zero.

The mechanism behind the second dimension is geometric. The comparison window is symmetric in calendar time, 182 days on each side of the release, but a benchmark's model coverage is not symmetric in calendar time, because coverage begins when the benchmark is built. A model evaluated shortly after its benchmark appeared is ranked almost entirely against models newer than itself, while one evaluated on a mature benchmark sees peers on both sides. \autoref{fig:geometry} draws both cases on one benchmark's coverage: the upper lane places a release at the start of that coverage, where the window reaches back into a stretch in which nothing was scored, and the lower lane places one in the interior, where the window fills on both sides. The two lanes differ in when the release shipped relative to the benchmark and in nothing else, which is what makes the composition of the peer set a property of timing rather than of the benchmark or the model.

The share of a cell's window released after the focal model is 0.4374 for eligible cells and 0.6320 for placebo cells, against 0.5 for a two-sided window, and within release the slope of standing on that share is $-29.09$ percentile points per unit among eligible cells alone. \autoref{fig:one-curve} plots standing against that share for both groups at once. Eligible and placebo cells do not form two clouds at different heights; they sit on a single declining curve and differ only in where along it they fall. A gap read as a difference between the groups is therefore a difference in position on one curve, which is what rules out reading it as disclosure behaviour.

Standing decomposes exactly into a weighted average of position among older and newer peers, $\text{percentile} = (1 - s)\,P_{\text{old}} + s\,P_{\text{new}}$ with $s$ the newer share, an identity that holds cell by cell to a maximum absolute error of $2.84\times10^{-14}$. Replacing the empirical weight with one half defines the side-balanced measure, available for 96.4\% of all cells and 96.5\% of eligible-or-placebo cells, and cuts the same slope to $-8.02$.

That the identity holds does not make the gradient an accounting tautology. Shuffling scores within benchmark destroys the capability trend while leaving dates, window membership, peer counts and newer shares bit-identical; under that null the slope has mean $-0.07$ and standard deviation 3.20, the observed $-29.09$ sits 9.1 standard deviations away, and none of 300 draws reaches it. Replacing scores instead with a within-benchmark linear fit on date plus permuted residuals, so that a capability trend exists and nothing else does, recovers 101\% of the observed slope, averaged over 200 draws. Comparison-set geometry is inert on its own and becomes a bias only when it multiplies a real trend in capability.

\subsection{Why trimming does not work}\label{sec:core-recentring}

The phenomenon has a name in nonparametric estimation: local averages are biased near the edge of a support because the neighbourhood is one-sided there, and the standard remedies recentre rather than discard \citep{gasser1979,fan1992,heckman1979}. What is unusual here is not the boundary but its coincidence with the endowment: a benchmark was available to a release exactly when the model did not sit at the left edge of that benchmark's coverage, both being the single statement that the benchmark existed first. The indicator of what a provider could have held is therefore the indicator of where the comparison window is one-sided, so the exclusion restriction that would make the placebo a control fails by construction, and removing the edge removes the contrast. That coincidence has an older name. Release date, benchmark vintage and the difference between them are exactly collinear, the age-period-cohort identity, and all three move standing here: $t_i$ through the capability trend, $\tau_b$ through saturation, $t_i - \tau_b$ through window composition. Only the linear components are unidentified in that structure \citep{fosse2019bounding,fosse2019review}, so what fails is a slope rather than every statement about $\theta$.

\autoref{tab:remedies} shows what follows. Trimming to two-sided windows moves the placebo gap from $+12.23$ to $+10.73$, removing 12\% of the contamination while discarding cells. Shortening the window does reduce the gap, to $+8.59$ at 91 days, but in proportion to the asymmetry it removes rather than by breaking the mechanism: across four widths and four benchmark-count minima the gap is positive in all sixteen specifications, from $+8.59$ to $+21.66$, and tracks the eligible-minus-placebo gap in window composition at $r = 0.989$. Dropping any one of the 18 organisations leaves it between $+11.45$ and $+13.20$. Ranking against every model ever scored, the intuitive alternative, roughly doubles the problem to $+22.75$, and raw scores are worse still, having no common unit across benchmarks. Conditioning removes 98.2\% of the same gap. The correction is a covariate, not a subsample.

One reading of that number would be too generous to it. Window composition is a function of the same dates that define the placebo indicator, so conditioning on it does not adjust for a confounder sitting outside the comparison. It splits the gap into the channel carrying it, which is what the label-free setting permits. The prescription that follows is narrower than the decomposition. A study that does hold disclosure labels and wants an estimate of $\theta$ must argue that window composition is fixed before the provider decides what to report; if a provider's choice responds to how a benchmark's peer set is composed, then controlling for it removes part of the quantity being measured. Nothing in this panel settles that.

The side-balanced percentile is the one change of measure that does substantial work, cutting the placebo mean to $+8.69$ and the eligible-cell gradient to $-8.02$. \autoref{fig:one-curve}(b) puts it against the uncorrected measure on the same cells, and it is close to flat over the range where cells are dense. Reweighting cannot reach what remains, since balancing repairs the weight placed on each side of a model but not which models an evaluator chose to score early, so evaluator attention stays a residual confound. Because it is undefined wherever a side is empty, we report it alongside the uncorrected measure rather than in place of it.
